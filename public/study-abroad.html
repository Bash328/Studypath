Studying abroad, honestly

Studying overseas is a real option for South African students — and it's also the area where you'll find the most confident-sounding wrong information online. This page explains how the process works and what to check. It deliberately doesn't give you specific entry requirements.

Why there are no numbers on this page

Everywhere else on Studypath, every requirement links to the official university page that states it. We haven't done that verification work for foreign universities yet, so rather than repeat what agents and forums say, we've left the specifics out.

What follows is the process, the things you need to line up, and the questions to ask. For the actual requirements, go to the university's own admissions page for international or South African applicants — and trust nothing else, including us, until you've seen it there.

There are two ways to do it

Route 1 — Apply directly to a foreign university

You apply as an international applicant, straight from matric, and do your whole degree overseas. This gives you the most choice and costs the most.

What this route usually involves:

  • An application through that country's system (some countries have one central application service for all universities; others have you apply to each one separately).
  • Getting your NSC assessed against that country's qualifications — every country does this differently, and it's the step people forget.
  • An English language test in some countries, even though you were taught in English. Whether you're exempt depends on the country and the university.
  • A student visa, which normally requires proof that you can pay for the whole thing.
  • Deadlines that are often a year earlier than you'd expect, sometimes before you write matric.

Route 2 — Start at a South African university and go abroad partway through

You register for a degree here, then spend a semester or a year at a partner university overseas through an exchange or study-abroad agreement. Your home university stays the one that gives you the degree.

Why a lot of students end up preferring this:

  • It's dramatically cheaper — you usually keep paying South African fees.
  • You apply once you're already a student, using your university results, so matric marks aren't the deciding factor.
  • Your degree is still a South African one, which matters for professional registration in fields like engineering, health and law.
  • The risk is lower: if it doesn't suit you, you come home to a degree you're already halfway through.

Most large South African universities run an international office that handles these agreements. Which partners exist, and who qualifies, differs by university and by faculty — ask that university's international office directly.

Questions worth asking before you commit

Will this degree be recognised back home?

If you plan to work in South Africa in a regulated profession — medicine, engineering, law, teaching, accounting — check with that profession's South African registration body before you enrol, not after you graduate.

What's the real cost over the full degree?

Compare the full multi-year total against the same degree here, including the rand exchange rate moving against you. International student fees are usually much higher than domestic ones.

Can you legally work while studying?

Student visa work rules vary a lot by country, and plans that depend on part-time income fall apart when the visa doesn't allow it.

Who's actually advising you?

Study-abroad agents are often paid commission by the universities they recommend. That doesn't make them dishonest, but it does mean you should verify everything on the university's own site.

A note on scams

Nobody legitimate will guarantee you admission, and nobody legitimate asks for a large upfront payment to "secure your place" before the university has made you an offer in writing. If a claim can't be found on the university's own website, treat it as false.
