The words everyone uses but nobody explains

If you've been told to "check your APS" and had no idea what that meant, you're not behind — nobody explains this properly. Here's the whole thing in plain language.

APS

Admission Point Score. A number universities work out from your Grade 11 or 12 marks to decide if you can get in. Higher is better.

The catch: every university calculates it differently. Some count 6 subjects, some count 7. Some include Life Orientation, some don't. Some don't use APS at all.

NSC achievement levels

Your percentage gets turned into a level from 1 to 7 (some universities use 1 to 8). Level 4 is 50–59%, level 5 is 60–69%, level 6 is 70–79%, level 7 is 80% and up.

When a university says "Maths level 6", it means 70% or more in Maths.

Home Language vs First Additional Language

Your Home Language is the one you're strongest in; your First Additional Language is your second one. Universities usually ask for a higher mark in a First Additional Language than a Home Language, because the subject is a bit easier.

The NBT

National Benchmark Tests. Extra tests some universities make you write on top of matric. They test reading, reasoning and maths — not your school syllabus.

Everything about the NBT →

"Selection programme"

Meeting the minimum does not get you in. The university picks the best applicants from everyone who qualified, so the real cut-off is usually higher than the published one. Medicine, engineering and law are almost always like this.

Bursary

Money for your studies that you usually don't pay back — unlike a loan. Some require you to work for the company afterwards. Deadlines are strict and most of them close mid-year.

Why you can trust the numbers here

We never guess a number

If we can't find a requirement on an official university page or PDF, we don't show it. We say we don't know instead. Every figure on this site has a link to its source next to it.

We don't mix up the universities' scores

A "Wits APS of 42" is not the same as a "UP APS of 35" or a "UCT score of 500". Putting them on one scale would be wrong, so we never do it. You get a separate result for each university.

We show you when sources disagree

Sometimes two official university pages give different numbers. We show you both and tell you they disagree, rather than quietly picking one and sounding confident.

We publish what we don't know

There's a public list of every gap in our data and why it's there. We're still adding universities — new ones go up twice a week as we verify them.
